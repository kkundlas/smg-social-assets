# Dialogue editing: complete-thought protocol

Treat dialogue editing as a semantic decision supported by transcript, waveform, audio, and picture. Silence detection is evidence, not an edit decision.

## Decision order

1. Normalize the transcript and terminology.
2. Group sentences into complete thoughts.
3. Detect candidate retakes and false starts.
4. Choose the strongest complete take.
5. Remove abandoned attempts at natural boundaries.
6. Tighten excessive pauses and filler.
7. Review continuity in audio and picture.
8. Re-read the edited paragraph for clinical and grammatical integrity.

## Retake taxonomy

- **Abandoned opening:** a sentence begins, stops, then restarts.
- **Correction:** a speaker replaces a fact, term, number, or formulation.
- **Duplicate take:** the same full thought is delivered more than once.
- **Partial retake:** only a clause is repeated to improve wording.
- **Performance retake:** meaning is identical but delivery quality differs.
- **Intentional repetition:** repetition used for emphasis or teaching.
- **List cadence:** repeated structure that helps listeners track items.

Do not remove intentional repetition or list cadence merely because transcript text is similar.

## Select the retained take

Prefer the take that is:

1. medically correct;
2. complete and grammatical;
3. clear and concise;
4. confident but natural;
5. visually usable;
6. easiest to join without clipped phonemes or facial discontinuity.

The final take is often best, but do not assume it automatically. If two complete takes give materially different medical advice, stop for a significant decision.

## Cut-boundary rules

- Cut at a sentence boundary whenever possible.
- Use a natural clause boundary only when both sides remain complete.
- Preserve consonant attacks, vowel tails, breaths, and room tone.
- Avoid cutting through blinks, large gestures, or mouth shape changes when the presenter remains visible.
- Use a relevant planned graphic or B-roll to cover an unavoidable picture jump.
- Do not manufacture a sentence by joining distant fragments unless its meaning and cadence are unquestionably intact.

## Pause and filler rules

Classify a pause before removing it:

- rhetorical emphasis;
- list separation;
- section transition;
- hesitation;
- technical dead air;
- post-CTA tail.

Retain enough pause to make the edit breathe. Do not apply a universal silence threshold. Remove a full dead tail after the sign-off, but preserve an intentional closing beat before it.

Remove filler only when its deletion improves clarity without creating a clipped or artificial join. A natural conversational word may remain when removal is more distracting than retention.

## Required cut-list evidence

Record:

- cut ID;
- source in and out;
- category;
- removed transcript;
- retained transcript or take;
- complete-thought check;
- medical-meaning check;
- audio-continuity check;
- picture-continuity check;
- coverage asset when needed;
- confidence and status.

## Four-part cut audit

Every committed cut must pass:

1. **Language:** the remaining sentence is complete and grammatical.
2. **Medicine:** no qualifier, warning, limitation, or causal relationship was lost.
3. **Sound:** the edit has natural room tone, breaths, and phoneme boundaries.
4. **Picture:** the edit does not expose a distracting jump; if it does, assign meaningful coverage.

## Do-not-cut examples

- A repeated phrase that introduces a deliberate contrast.
- A pause before “talk to your physician” that gives the advice appropriate weight.
- Similar list openings that orient the viewer.
- A self-correction that changes the medical meaning unless the correct full take is clearly identified.
- A partial sentence when the later restart depends on context contained only in the first attempt.

## Escalation threshold

Ask only when the correct retained meaning cannot be determined safely. Do not ask for routine retake cleanup when one complete, medically equivalent take is clearly stronger.
